Technical background

How OGS-TS works, and why it's built this way

2026-11-11 · Companion to the OGS-TS v1 announcement

The OGS Telemetry Standard (OGS-TS) lets a game describe what is happening in it once, on the local network, so any device listening can understand it. This post is for developers deciding whether to adopt it: how it is built, what the design choices are, and what evidence stands behind it. The normative details are in the standard's repository.

A pattern motorsport already solved

Race cars have carried dozens of sensors over a shared bus for decades. The trick is the CAN bus with a DBC file: declare the signal dictionary once, then stream bare values. Nobody puts the signal name and unit on every sample.

OGS-TS applies the same idea to games. The standard was first committed in April 2024, and was previously published as the Local Telemetry Standard.

Three frames, one multicast group

Everything travels as UDP multicast on the local network (default 239.255.17.17:40123), so any number of receivers can join with no per-device setup.

  • Discovery, every two seconds: the game, its objects, and a dictionary of channels. Each channel is declared once with an id, name, type, unit and range. A receiver that joins late has the full picture within two seconds.
  • Motion, at the game's telemetry rate: the bare samples, keyed by the ids from discovery. 60 Hz is typical, not a ceiling. A pose-only frame is about 70 bytes.
  • Event, when something happens: impacts, gunfire and other discrete stimuli, with replay and deduplication so a lossy network cannot drop them.

Games declare their own channels. A rafting game declares what a raft has, not what a car has, and receivers skip channels they don't recognise. Shared names live in the Common Channels annex, and a new entry needs evidence from at least two games.

One canonical encoding, no runtime libraries

Frames are Cap'n Proto, chosen because decoders can skip fields they don't recognise. The schema can grow without breaking receivers already deployed.

OGS-TS goes a step further than ordinary Cap'n Proto: normative encoding rules define one canonical byte layout per frame, and every implementation must produce byte-identical output. Frame writers are generated from the schema for both C# and C++. None of the SDKs needs a Cap'n Proto runtime library, so they carry no third-party dependencies and build wherever their engine builds: Windows, Linux, macOS, ARM, and ahead-of-time platforms such as IL2CPP.

A shared conformance corpus checks every implementation in continuous integration, byte for byte. The corpus itself is independently verified with the official Cap'n Proto tools. If you implement OGS-TS in another language, the corpus tells you exactly when you are done.

Conventions, settled once

Steering direction, axis orientation, radians or degrees: these are the questions every consumer used to learn per game. CONVENTIONS.md settles them for every sender. The SDKs share one unit-conversion table and convert at bind time, so a publisher written for any engine produces frames any receiver can consume.

Evidence

A schema is only useful if real games fit in it. OGS-TS has been mapped against more than a hundred documented game outputs across sixteen validation exercises, covering official SDKs, community reverse-engineered offsets, vendor-encapsulated integrations and game-native UDP outputs. There are no open gaps: every source field fits.

  • Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024: all 1,354 exposed variables mapped, with a sender reference.
  • iRacing: 250 documented variables plus session data, with a sender reference.

It also runs in shipped games. Three loader mods add OGS-TS to Unity and Unreal Engine titles that don't ship it, with no changes to the games:

The mods follow one rule from the standard: the motion object is the pawn the player controls, not the camera. Engines smooth cameras, and a camera would feed the rig's motion back into the view. The pawn is the rigid body a motion platform is trying to reproduce.

Safe by design

  • Read-only. Receivers cannot send anything back to the game, so the stream cannot be used to cheat.
  • Local. Telemetry never leaves the player's network. No cloud, no account.

Getting started

  • Making a game: ogs-ts-unity (2022.3+ and Unity 6, Mono and IL2CPP), ogs-ts-unreal, ogs-ts-godot (Godot 4 .NET), or ogs-ts-dotnet for a custom .NET engine.
  • Building hardware or receiver software: the receiving library and reference receiver in ogs-ts-dotnet, then IMPLEMENTING.md.
  • Checking a stream: the OGS-TS Monitor shows every sender on your network and flags anything that doesn't conform.
  • Questions: Discord.
